## How Your Authors Form a Single Continuous Story

If you read these topics chronologically, you will witness one of the greatest intellectual arcs in human history:

[Math Foundations] [Computability Theory] [Automated Theorem Proving] [Prolog Language]
Grassmann / Dedekind / Peano --> Hilbert's Decision Problem --> Turing & Church Prove Limits --> Davis-Putnam / Robinson --> Kowalski & Van Emden
(What is a number?) (Can a machine solve math?) (What can code actually do?) (Computers find proofs) (Logic becomes code)

1. The Foundations Era (1861–1910): Grassmann, Peirce, Dedekind, and Peano try to strip human intuition away from math, reducing arithmetic to raw, symbolic rules. Zermelo and Fraenkel fix the cracks by building formal Set Theory.
2. The Crisis & Computability Era (1900–1937): David Hilbert challenges the world to prove that math is complete and decidable. Gödel shatters this dream in 1931 by proving some truths can never be proved. Turing and Church pick up the pieces, defining exactly what a "computable machine" can and cannot calculate.
3. The Automation Era (1949–1965): Julia Robinson, Davis, Putnam, and J.A. Robinson realize that even if computers can't solve all math, they can use first-order logic to prove incredibly complex theorems automatically using Resolution. [7]
4. The Programming Era (1970s): Kowalski and Van Emden realize that these exact automated proofs can be structured as data queries, giving birth to Prolog (Programming in Logic)—bringing your journey full circle from mathematical philosophy straight into practical computer code. [7]

## 🗺️ Chapter 1 Overview: Sentential Logic

Chapter 1 focuses on Sentential Logic (also known as Propositional Logic). This is the study of how simple sentences connect using words like AND, OR, and IF-THEN.
While this sounds simple, Enderton uses this chapter to lay down the entire structural blueprint you will need for the rest of the curriculum.

---

## 🔑 Key Concepts to Master

As you read, focus your attention on mastering these four core pillars:

1. The Formal Language (Syntax):

- What it is: Enderton defines exactly what counts as a legal "sentence" in math. He introduces parameters (like A, B, C) and connective symbols ($\neg, \to$).
  - Why it matters: You cannot let human intuition slip in. A computer or a formal system only cares about the shape of the symbols, not your feelings about them.

2. Induction and Recursion:

- What it is: This is a crucial mathematical tool used to define an infinite number of sentences using a few simple generation rules.
  - Connection to your list: This is the modern formalization of Hermann Grassmann (1861) and Richard Dedekind's (1888) breakthroughs on how to define numbers and arithmetic via induction.

3. Truth Assignments (Semantics):

- What it is: This is where we bring meaning to the symbols. Enderton introduces a function v that assigns a value of either T (True) or F (False) to our symbols.
  - Connection to your list: This lays the foundational framework that Alfred Tarski (1926–1928) later expanded into full Model Theory to define absolute truth in languages.

4. Tautologies and Truth Tables:

- What it is: A tautology is a statement that is always true, no matter what values you plug into it (for example: "A or not A").
  - Connection to your list: Recognizing tautologies automatically is the exact problem that Davis, Putnam, and J.A. Robinson solved decades later with computer code.

---

## ⚠️ Speed Bumps & Mental Traps to Watch For

Enderton is highly rigorous, which means beginners often trip over a few specific mathematical formalisms:

- The Arrow Symbol (→): In everyday English, "If P, then Q" implies that P causes Q. In mathematical logic, it does not. The statement "1+1=3 → The moon is made of cheese" is considered True by Enderton's definitions simply because the first part is false. This is called material implication. Accept it as a strict mathematical rule, not a linguistic one!
- Unique Readability: Enderton spends a lot of time proving that parentheses ensure a formula can only be read one way (e.g., $(A \land B) \to C$ is different from $A \land (B \to C)$). It feels tedious to prove this mathematically, but remember your end goal: computers. A computer compiler will crash if a line of code has two ambiguous meanings.
- The Metacharacter Trap: Pay close attention when Enderton uses Greek letters (like α, β, φ). These are not symbols inside the logic language itself. They are "metavariables"—symbols used by us (the humans) to talk about the logic language. Keeping the language and the "metalanguage" separate is the secret key to understanding Gödel's Incompleteness Theorem later on.

## 🛠️ Recommended Exercises to Complete

Do not just read Enderton passively; logic is a muscle. When you reach the end of the sections in Chapter 1, ensure you can confidently solve these types of problems:

- The Induction Proofs: Try the exercises asking you to prove properties of sentences by induction (e.g., proving that every well-formed formula has an equal number of left and right parentheses).
- Truth Table Construction: Practice building truth tables for complex formulas to determine if they are tautologies, satisfiable (can be true), or unsatisfiable (always false).
- English-to-Symbol Translation: Pay special attention to translating tricky phrases like "P unless Q" or "P is necessary for Q" into formal symbols.
